Introduction
The global prevalence of Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection remains above 40% [1]. H. pylori infection,
which invariably causes gastritis, is difficult to clear naturally unless treated with eradication therapies
consisting of antibiotics and acid suppressants [2]. Persistent infection can lead to chronic atrophic gas-
tritis, peptic ulcers, gastric cancer, gastric mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma etc. H. pylori has

also been linked to an increased risk of colorectal cancer and Alzheimer’s disease [3,4]. However, the rise
in antibiotic resistance, fueled by widespread antibiotic use, has caused H. pylori eradication rate to
decline [5]. Additionally, H. pylori eradication therapies also induce an increase in long-term resistant
bacteria communities in the gut [6].
   Probiotics, live microorganisms that bring health benefits, have emerged as a potential adjunct to H.
pylori therapy. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and meta-analyses suggest that probiotics can reduce
side effects associated with H. pylori eradication treatments, though their impact on H. pylori eradication
rate remains unclear [7–14]. Emerging evidence highlights the gut microbiome’s role in H. pylori clear-
ance. Probiotics may inhibit H. pylori growth through producing antibacterial substances, competing for
ecological niches, and modulating the immune system [15–18]. Probiotics also modulate the microbiome
across the digestive tract [19–22], potentially altering the environment in which H. pylori thrives and
leading to the decolonization of pathogenic bacteria [23]. Furthermore, faecal microbiota transplantation
(FMT) has even been reported to clear H. pylori in some cases, underscoring the microbiome’s impor-
tance [24].
   Despite these findings, it remains unclear whether using probiotics before H. pylori eradication therapy
improves outcomes. This study aims to address this gap by evaluating the efficacy of probiotics admin-
istered prior to H. pylori treatment through a systematic review and meta-analysis.


Methodology
Registration of review protocol
This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for
Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guideline [25]. The study protocol was registered on the
International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD42021233382; 22 Feb 2021; Updated 07
Jan 2024).


Search strategy
A comprehensive literature search of PubMed, EMBASE, the Cochrane Library, and the Conference
Proceedings Citation Index was carried out up till January 2024 with the following search strategy
(Helicobacter pylori OR H. pylori OR Campylobacter Pylori) AND (probiotic OR probiotics OR yeast OR yeasts
OR yogurt OR Lactobacillus OR Bifidobacterium OR Saccharomyces OR Streptococcus OR Enterococcus OR
Bacillus OR prebiotics OR Lactococcus OR fermented milk OR Clostridium butyricum OR Bacillus subtilis OR
Bacillus licheniformis OR Curd). We also manually checked the reference lists of RCTs and systematic
reviews identified by previous searches to include other potentially eligible studies.


Eligibility criteria
The title and abstract were used to assess the eligibility of the records. Eligible records were screened
for duplicates. Data were extracted from these eligible articles independently by two authors (TM and
SX), and any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (ZY). RCTs that com-
pared the pretreatment of probiotics for H. pylori eradication (pretreatment group) with current main-
stream guideline-recommended therapies (control group) [7,26], with or without the use of probiotics,
were included. Reviews, meta-analyses, comments, letters, case reports, case series, animal studies, and
studies without controls were excluded. Additionally, studies that did not report the H. pylori eradication
rate, used serological tests to diagnose active H. pylori infection, had an interval of less than 4 weeks for
H. pylori re-check, or did not report the interval were also excluded.


Risk of bias assessment
Two investigators (TM and ZY) separately rated the risk of bias in the included RCTs according to the risk of
bias assessment tool (RevMan 5.4, Nordic Cochrane Center, Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark).
                                                                                      Annals of Medicine      3


The assessment covered the following bias domains: random sequence generation (selection bias), allocation
concealment (selection bias), blinding of participants and personnel (performance bias), blinding of outcome
assessment (detection bias), incomplete outcome data (attrition bias), selective reporting (reporting bias),
and others. Each domain was categorized as having a low, high, or uncertain risk of bias. If RCTs ≥ 10 was
included in the meta-analysis, funnel plots were constructed to assess the risk of publication bias.


Data extraction
We extracted the following information from the included studies: (a) year of publication, region, first
author, study design, participants’ characteristics, number of patients in each treatment arm, methods
used to confirm H. pylori infection; (b) the type, dose, and duration of probiotics used; (c) initial or rescue
therapy, regimen of eradication, eradication rates, side effects, number of patients in whom H. pylori
infection was successfully eradicated (either provided directly or calculated from the intention-to-treat
[ITT] and the per-protocol [PP] analyses).


Data synthesis and analysis
The endpoints of interest in this meta-analysis were the H. pylori eradication rate. To evaluate the het-
erogeneity among the included studies, the inconsistency index (I2) statistic was performed. An I2 value
larger than 50% indicated a high degree of heterogeneity, and a random-effects model was used to pool
studies for analyses. Otherwise, a fixed-effects model was used [27]. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed
using sensitivity analyses to determine whether any clinical heterogeneity contributed to such a statisti-
cal difference.
   Both ITT and PP analyses were used to determine the clinical outcomes. Subgroup analyses were
performed to compare the H. pylori eradication rates across studies from different countries, eradication
regimens, H. pylori group, probiotic types (single probiotic strains or combinations of probiotics), dura-
tions of probiotics used before H. pylori eradication therapies, and study designs of probiotics pretreat-
ment. We summed dichotomous outcome measures as the risk ratio (RR) along with 95% confidence
interval (CI) using RevMan 5.4. The results of this meta-analysis were reported as forest plots.


Results
Study selection
A flow diagram of this study is presented in Figure 1. Initially, 867 articles were identified through the
primary literature search. Of these, 605 were excluded due to being duplicates, irrelevant to the analysis,
or not RCTs. A total of 262 articles were retrieved for further review.
   Eight studies were excluded because they were either incomplete or unpublished after registration.
153 articles were excluded as they did not involve the use of probiotics prior to H. pylori eradication
treatment. Further exclusions included 52 articles that lacked current mainstream guideline-recommended
therapies as controls, 29 articles that did not involve probiotics, and two similar studies published by
authors from the same institution within a year. Two articles were excluded for not reporting the H. pylori
eradication rate, and two others were excluded because the H. pylori re-check interval was either less
than four weeks or not reported. One article was excluded as it was a conference report, and the final
report of the same study had already been published. Another article was excluded for using non-viable
probiotics. Finally, twelve studies involving 2,144 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in
the meta-analysis [28–39].


Characteristics of eligible studies
The characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1. All studies involved adults, with
no pediatric participants. Six studies from China included 1,156 patients, while six studies from Japan,
the United States, Italy, Iraq, Iran, and the UAE included 988 patients. The studies covered both initial

and rescue therapies for H. pylori infection, with four studies not specifying the therapy type and one

Outcomes of overall eradication rate
The eradication rate of H. pylori was evaluated across twelve studies involving 2,144 patients. By ITT
analysis, the pooled eradication rate was 80.34% (707/880) in the pretreatment group and 70.49%
(891/1,264) in the control group. Pretreatment with probiotics significantly improved the eradication rate
(RR = 1.14, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.19, p < 0.001), with low heterogeneity observed between studies (I2 = 36%)
(Figure 2). Similarly, PP analysis revealed a pooled eradication rate of 86.43% (707/818) in the pretreat-
ment group and 76.88% (891/1,159) in the control group. Probiotics pretreatment also improved the
eradication rate (RR = 1.12, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.17, p < 0.001), although heterogeneity was higher (I2 = 57%)
(Figure 3).

Subgroup analysis for H. pylori eradication rate by region
In ITT analysis, studies from China (6 studies) showed a higher eradication rate in the pretreatment
group (RR = 1.14, 95% CI 1.07 to 1.22, p < 0.001, I2 = 0%), while studies outside China (6 studies) also
demonstrated higher rates (RR = 1.13, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.22, p < 0.001, I2 = 60%). In PP analysis, studies
from China reported similar results (RR = 1.13, 95% CI 1.07 to 1.20, p < 0.001, I2 =0%), whereas studies
outside China showed higher rates (RR = 1.11, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.18, p < 0.001, I2 = 74%). Both sub-
groups had consistent findings, with lower heterogeneity in studies from China (Table 2, Supplement
Figures S1–S2).

Subgroup analysis for H. pylori eradication rate by eradication regimen
In ITT analysis, standard triple therapy (6 studies) showed a higher eradication rate in the pretreatment
group (RR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.07–1.23; I2 = 0%), while the bismuth quadruple therapy (6 studies) also
demonstrated higher rates (RR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.05–1.21; I2 = 66%). In PP analysis, standard triple therapy
yielded similar results (RR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.08–1.22; I2 = 0%), whereas bismuth quadruple therapy showed
slightly lower rates (RR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.04–1.16; I2 = 75%). Both regimens were effective (Table 2,
Supplement Figures S3–S4).

Subgroup analysis for H. pylori eradication rate by H. pylori group
In ITT analysis, consistent results were observed across different H. pylori groups (initial therapy, rescue
therapy, and unclear). The initial H. pylori group (2 studies) exhibited a higher eradication rate in the
pretreatment group (RR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.05 to 1.30; I2 = 0%). The rescue H. pylori group (5 studies) also
showed increased eradication rates (RR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.06 to 1.23; I2 = 12%). Similarly, the unclear H.
pylori group (5 studies) demonstrated higher efficacy (RR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.02 to 1.21; I2 = 67%). PP
analysis reported similar results (Table 2, Supplement Figures S5–S6).


Subgroup analysis for H. pylori eradication rate by probiotic types
In ITT analysis, the combination of probiotics (8 studies) showed a higher eradication rate in the pretreat-
ment group (RR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.07–1.23; I2 = 20%), while a certain probiotic (4 studies) also demon-
strated higher rates (RR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.05–1.20; I2 = 63%). In PP analysis, the combination of probiotics
yielded similar results (RR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.05–1.18; I2 = 53%), whereas the certain probiotic showed
comparable rates (RR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.07–1.20; I2 = 73%). Both probiotic types were effective (Table 2,
Supplement Figures S7–S8).

Subgroup analysis for H. pylori eradication rate by probiotics pretreatment duration
In ITT analysis, the duration of probiotics pretreatment prior to H. pylori eradication therapy yielded
inconsistent results. Pretreatment for 3 to 7 days did not significantly improve the eradication rate
(RR = 1.06, 95% CI 0.93 to 1.22, p = 0.39, I2 = 89%). However, pretreatment for 14 days significantly
enhanced the eradication rate (RR = 1.14, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.21, p < 0.001, I2 = 5%). Similarly, pretreatment
for 21 days or longer also improved the eradication rate (RR = 1.16, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.28, p = 0.003,
I2 = 0%). PP analysis reported similar results. (Table 2, Supplement Figures S9–S10).


Subgroup analysis for H. pylori eradication rate by study design
In ITT analysis, significant improvements were observed in pre vs. free (RR = 1.21, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.31,
p < 0.001, I2 = 25%) and pre and combine vs. free (RR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.12–1.36; I2 = 0%), while other study
designs with fewer studies did not show significant effects. In PP analysis, pre vs. free (RR = 1.20, 95% CI:
1.11 to1.29; I2 = 16%) and pre and combine vs. free (RR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.11 to1.32; I2 = 0%) remained
significant, with other designs yielding non-significant findings. Pretreatment before H. pylori eradication
therapy showed consistent efficacy, particularly in comparison to free regimens (Table 2, Supplement
Figures S11–S12).


Side effects
The main reported side effects included nausea, diarrhea, constipation and metallic taste et al. Some
studies reported that side effects were similar, and some studies did not report side effects. According
to available data from eight studies (1551 patients), side effects were less likely in the probiotics pretreat-
ment group compared to the control group (16.0% vs. 28.3%, RR = 0.59, 95% CI 0.41 to 0.84, p < 0.005,
Figure 4).


Risk of bias and sensitivity analysis
The risk of bias summary was shown in Figure 5. The risks for bias were shown in Supplement Figure S13.
In the sensitivity analysis, after deleting one study at a time, the statistical significance of the combined
RR remained unchanged.


Publication bias
Funnel plot showed no significant publication bias among the twelve studies included in this meta-analysis
(Supplement Figure S14).

4. Discussion
This systematic review and meta-analysis investigated the efficacy of probiotics pretreatment in improv-
ing H. pylori eradication rate. Unlike previous studies, which primarily focused on probiotics administered
during or after H. pylori eradication therapies, this study explored the role of probiotics as a pretreatment
strategy. Probiotics pretreatment significantly increased eradication rate (80.34% vs. 70.49%, RR = 1.14,
95% CI 1.08–1.19, p < 0.001). Subgroup analyses confirmed benefits across regions and eradication regi-
mens, H. pylori group, with both single and combined probiotics showing efficacy. However, pretreat-
ment duration was critical: 14 days or longer improved eradication rates, while shorter durations (3–7 days)
did not. Study designs pre vs. free and pre and combine vs. free further validated probiotic pretreatment’s
efficacy in improving H. pylori eradication rate. These results suggest that probiotics, when administered
prior to H. pylori eradication therapy, may play a beneficial role in improving treatment outcomes.
   Two previous meta-analyses examined the efficacy of probiotics administered at different timings
(before, during, after) in H. pylori eradication therapy. Lv et al. found that probiotics used before and after
the treatment regimen improved eradication rates [40], while Shi et al. reported that probiotics adminis-
tered before and throughout eradication therapy enhanced efficacy [10]. These meta-analyses included 4
or 5 RCTs with probiotics used before H. pylori eradication, and 1or 2 RCTs did not meet the current
study’s eligibility criteria; and their findings align with our study results. These findings contrast with
earlier research, which suggested that while certain probiotics, such as Lactobacillus, can inhibit H. pylori
growth, adhesion, and colonization through mechanisms like autolysin secretion and reduced binding to
glycolipid receptors,16-18 they do not clear H. pylori infection on their own [41]. For instance, single pro-
biotic strains have been shown to reduce urease activity in H. pylori-infected patients but fail to eradicate
the pathogen [21,42].

   The potential mechanisms underlying the benefits of probiotics pretreatment may involve biofilm for-
mation and gut microbiome modulation. Some probiotics can bind to and produce biofilms in cultured
cells, significantly reducing H. pylori binding and colonization [43,44]. Additionally, supplementing with
probiotics alters the gut microbiome, which may create an environment less conducive to H. pylori sur-
vival [19–21]. This is supported by studies showing that FMT cleared H. pylori in 40.6% of patients, high-
lighting the gut microbiome’s potential role in H. pylori treatment [24]. Furthermore, a meta-analysis
involving patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), a condition associated with significant gut
microbiome changes, found a reduced H. pylori infection rate in IBD patients, further emphasizing the
interplay between the gut microbiome and H. pylori clearance [45].

and immune function, offering hope for the treatment of infectious diseases. For example, FMT has proven

effective against Clostridium difficile infection and shows promise in treating human immunodeficiency virus
infection [46–48]. Similarly, probiotic supplementation has been associated with reduced mortality in
COVID-19 patients and may help decolonize pathogenic bacteria from the gut [49]. These findings, along
with our results, suggest that modulating the gut microbiome—whether through probiotics, FMT, or other
methods—could be a valuable strategy for addressing multidrug-resistant H. pylori and the long-term resis-
tant bacterial communities that arise from eradication therapies. However, the mechanisms underlying these
effects, such as the migration of gut-licensed immune cells to other tissues, require further investigation [50].
    Despite these promising findings, this study has several limitations. First, the meta-analysis included
only twelve RCTs with varying probiotic strains, doses, durations, and combinations, which may affect the
reliability of the conclusions. Second, six of the included studies were conducted in China, and although
studies from other regions supported the same conclusions, more diverse populations are needed to
generalize the findings. Third, this study did not evaluate the simultaneous use of probiotics during
eradication therapies or their supplementation after treatment, as these topics have been extensively
covered in previous umbrella meta-analyses [9]. Future research should investigate key factors affecting
probiotic efficacy, including strain selection, dosage optimization, antimicrobial interactions, and ethnic/
age-specific responses through rigorous clinical trials and omics technologies.
    In conclusion, this meta-analysis provides evidence that probiotics pretreatment significantly improves
H. pylori eradication rate and reduces side effects, particularly when probiotics is used for an adequate
duration. These findings highlight the potential value of gut microbiome modulation in H. pylori treat-
ment and offer a new direction for addressing the challenges of antibiotic resistance and treatment
failure. Further studies are needed to elucidate the mechanisms involved and to develop standardized
protocols for probiotic use in H. pylori eradication therapies.
